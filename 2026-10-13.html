Return

Brown thornbill back after 31 days unheard

A single afternoon call marks the return of a small mimic, while 15 species and 244 calls fill the 24 hours to 9:30 pm.

The Brown Thornbill is back. It was heard once, at 2:11 pm today, after 31 days without a call. It has now turned up on 2 of the 55 days the station has been listening.

It is one of the most widespread birds around Canberra and lives in the region year-round, so the long gap is a gap in detection rather than in presence. Still, one call in a whole day is a slight way to mark a return.

The species is a skilled mimic, copying the alarm calls of other birds such as the New Holland honeyeater to deter predators. Today it offered no imitations for the microphone, just the one call, and then nothing more before press time.

The Dawn Chorus

The chorus began in darkness with a Pied Currawong at 6:00 am, three minutes after first light and 24 minutes before sunrise. An Australian Magpie followed at 6:05 am, then a Silvereye at 6:12 am. The Yellow-rumped Thornbill called at 6:21 am, two minutes before the sun came up at 6:24 am. A quieter stretch followed, until the Magpie-lark at 7:06 am, 43 minutes after sunrise. The Weebill arrived at 7:29 am and the Spotted Pardalote at 7:40 am. By 7:54 am, when the dawn window closed, all seven had been heard.

The Regular

Weebill

The Weebill kept to a short morning shift: four calls, the first at 7:29 am and the last at 10:57 am. That opening call came 65 minutes after sunrise. It has been heard on 39 of the 55 days the station has been listening.

Small enough to be mistaken for something blown in on the wind, the weebill forages busily in pairs or small parties of up to 8, often hovering and clinging to twigs to glean insects from the outer foliage. Its song is a loud, clear, musical pee-pee p'wee, often heard from a considerable distance.
