Olive-backed Oriole fills the morning with eleven calls

A summer migrant gives the microphone its busiest day of oriole song yet, while the chorus runs from first light to well past sunrise.

The Olive-backed Oriole had its busiest day yet in the 24 hours to 8:30 am today, with eleven calls heard. The previous best was 4 calls in a day. All eleven came in a single minute of the dawn: the first at 6:46 am, the last at 6:47 am.

It was heard seventeen minutes after sunrise, closing a stretch of morning song that began with the Silvereye and ran through the Pied Currawong, Australian Magpie, Magpie-lark and Eurasian Blackbird before the oriole joined it.

The bird is a summer migrant to the Canberra region, most often recorded in October, November and December, and is a moderately common bird around Canberra. The species has been heard on 5 of the 51 days the station has been listening. It is not bright in colour, with an olive back marked by small dark streaks.

The Regular

Magpie-lark

The Magpie-lark gave five calls in the window, the first at 10:52 am yesterday and the last at 7:41 am today, with its dawn call at 6:37 am. It is one of only about 200 bird species worldwide that sing in duet, with partners singing half a second apart.

It lives in the Canberra region year-round and is one of the most widespread birds around Canberra, heard on 37 of the 51 days the station has been listening.

Did You Know?

  • Olive-backed Oriole. The species was originally described in the genus Coracias by the English ornithologist John Latham in 1801. [source]
  • Olive-backed Oriole. Unlike the green oriole, it prefers more open woodland environments and tolerates drier climates, though not the desert. [source]
  • Magpie-lark. Pairs sing duets together to defend their territory, and sing most vigorously at strangers rather than familiar neighbours. [source]
